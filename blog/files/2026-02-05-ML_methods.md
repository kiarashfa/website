In the [previous post](https://kiarashfa.github.io/website/blog/?post=ml-paradigms), I walked through the major paradigms in machine learning: supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised, reinforcement, and transfer learning. Those paradigms describe *how* a model learns, the relationship between the model and its data. But they do not tell you what the model actually is under the hood. A neural network can be trained in a supervised fashion or an unsupervised one. A random forest is typically supervised, but the features it operates on might come from an unsupervised preprocessing step. The paradigm is the philosophy; the method is the machinery.

This post covers that machinery. I will walk through the most important families of machine learning methods, what they are, how they work, and where they shine. As before, I will draw on examples from polymer science where it helps make things concrete, but the methods themselves are universal. ## Neural Networks: The Foundation

### Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)

Artificial neural networks are the foundation on which most of modern deep learning is built. The basic idea is inspired (loosely) by biological neurons: you have layers of interconnected nodes, where each connection has a weight, and each node applies a simple nonlinear function to its inputs. Data enters through the input layer, flows through one or more hidden layers where it gets transformed, and comes out through the output layer as a prediction.

What makes neural networks so powerful is their flexibility. With enough neurons and enough layers, a neural network can approximate virtually any function. This is not just a handwaving claim; it is backed by the universal approximation theorem, which states that a sufficiently wide neural network with a single hidden layer can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy. In practice, we usually use multiple layers (hence "deep" learning) because depth tends to be more parameter-efficient than width for capturing complex, hierarchical patterns.

Training a neural network means adjusting all those connection weights so that the network's predictions match the known outputs as closely as possible. This is done through [backpropagation](https://doi.org/10.1038/323533a0), where the error at the output is propagated backward through the network, and each weight is nudged in the direction that reduces the error. It is essentially gradient descent operating on a very high-dimensional landscape, and it works remarkably well for an approach that seems like it should get hopelessly stuck in local minima.
 
The plain feedforward neural network is a general-purpose workhorse. It works for regression, classification, and a wide range of structured data problems. In polymer science, for instance, we used a feedforward ANN trained on over 5,000 monomer pairs to [predict reactivity ratios](https://doi.org/10.1039/D3PY00246B) purely from molecular structure descriptors (molecular fingerprints). These ratios govern how monomers incorporate into copolymer chains, and the ANN significantly outperformed the classical Q-e scheme that the field had used for decades. It was a straightforward supervised regression task: feed in the molecular fingerprints, predict the ratios. No physics, no kinetic equations, just patterns extracted from data.

But this general-purpose nature is also a limitation. The vanilla ANN does not have any built-in assumptions about the structure of the input data. It treats each input feature independently, with no notion of spatial arrangement, temporal ordering, or sequential dependencies. For many problems, that agnosticism is fine. For others, you need something more specialized.

### Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)

Convolutional neural networks are designed specifically for data that has spatial structure, most famously images, but the principle extends to anything with a meaningful local arrangement. The key innovation is the convolutional layer, which applies small filters (also called kernels) that slide across the input, detecting local patterns like edges, corners, and textures. Because the same filter is applied everywhere across the image, the network automatically learns features that are translation-invariant, meaning it can recognize a pattern regardless of where it appears.

A typical CNN stacks multiple convolutional layers, with each successive layer learning increasingly abstract features. The first layer might detect simple edges. The second might combine those edges into shapes. Deeper layers might recognize complex objects or textures. After the convolutional layers extract these spatial features, the output is typically flattened and passed through fully connected layers for the final prediction.

CNNs [revolutionized computer vision](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1989.1.4.541), but their applications extend far beyond photographs. In polymer and materials science, CNNs have been applied to analyze microscopy images of particle morphologies, classify microstructures from TEM or SEM images, and process spectral data that has been reformatted into image-like representations. Even one-dimensional signals like NIR spectra, Raman spectra, or GPC chromatograms can benefit from 1D convolutions that detect local patterns along the signal. The key question to ask is: does my data have a meaningful spatial or local arrangement where nearby values are related? If yes, a CNN is worth considering.

### Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)

While CNNs handle spatial structure, recurrent neural networks are built for sequential data, where the order of the inputs matters. Think of time series, language, or any process that unfolds over time.

The defining feature of an RNN is its memory. Unlike a feedforward network that processes each input independently, an RNN maintains a hidden state that carries information from previous time steps into the current one. At each step, the network takes in the current input along with the hidden state from the previous step, processes them together, and produces both an output and an updated hidden state. This creates a flow of information through time, allowing the network to learn temporal patterns and dependencies.

In practice, basic RNNs struggle with long sequences because the gradient signal tends to vanish (or explode) as it is propagated back through many time steps. This gave rise to more sophisticated variants like LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory networks) and GRUs (Gated Recurrent Units), which use gating mechanisms to control what information is remembered and what is forgotten. These variants are far more effective at capturing long-range dependencies, and they are the ones you will actually encounter in most practical applications today.

For process engineering, RNNs are a natural fit. Chemical processes are inherently dynamic: the state of a reactor at any given moment depends on its history. If you want to predict how a polymer's molecular weight distribution will evolve over the course of a semi-batch reaction, you need a model that understands the sequence of events that led to the current state: what monomers were fed at what rate, what the temperature profile looked like, when the initiator was added. An RNN can take in this time series of process measurements (temperatures, calorimetric signals, feed rates at each control interval) and learn to predict the resulting polymer properties, incorporating the memory of everything that happened before. This sequential awareness is what makes RNNs so natural for dynamic process modeling.

### Autoencoders

Autoencoders are a fascinating class of neural networks that are trained to reconstruct their own input. That might sound pointless until you realize the trick: the network is forced to pass the data through a bottleneck, a much lower-dimensional intermediate representation called the latent space. To reconstruct the input accurately from this compressed representation, the encoder half of the network must learn to capture the most essential features of the data, and the decoder half must learn to reconstruct the full picture from just those features.

The architecture is symmetric: encoder on one side, decoder on the other, latent space in the middle. During training, the model learns to minimize the difference between the input and the reconstruction. Whatever survives the compression bottleneck is, by definition, the most important information in the data.

Autoencoders have several important uses. Dimensionality reduction is the most direct: the latent space representation gives you a compressed version of your data that preserves its most important characteristics, often better than linear methods like PCA for complex, nonlinear datasets. In [our own work](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compchemeng.2024.108739) on reinforcement learning for controlling emulsion polymerization, we used an autoencoder to compress particle morphology distributions into a compact latent representation. The RL agent could then work in this low-dimensional space rather than dealing with the full distribution directly, and we decoded back to the full representation when we needed it. Without the autoencoder, the state space would have been too large for the RL agent to learn efficiently.

Beyond dimensionality reduction, autoencoders are also used for denoising (training the model to reconstruct clean data from noisy inputs, forcing it to learn what the "true" signal looks like), anomaly detection (data points that are poorly reconstructed are likely anomalies, because the model has never learned to compress that kind of pattern), and generative modeling. Variational autoencoders (VAEs), a probabilistic extension, can generate entirely new data points by sampling from the latent space, which has exciting applications in molecular design and materials discovery. You can, in principle, sample a point in the latent space and decode it into a candidate molecule with desired properties.

### Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

GANs are one of the most creative ideas in modern machine learning. A GAN consists of two neural networks that are trained simultaneously in competition with each other: a generator and a discriminator.

The generator's job is to produce synthetic data that looks as realistic as possible. It starts from random noise sampled from a simple distribution and transforms it into something that resembles real data. The discriminator's job is to tell real data apart from the generator's fakes. During training, the generator gets better at fooling the discriminator, and the discriminator gets better at catching fakes. This adversarial dynamic pushes both networks to improve, and when training goes well, the generator eventually produces data that the discriminator cannot reliably distinguish from reality.

The elegance of GANs lies in the fact that you never have to explicitly define what "realistic" means. The discriminator learns to recognize realism from examples, and the generator learns to produce it by trying to fool an increasingly sophisticated judge. This implicit learning of data distributions has produced spectacular results in image generation, but the applications in science are equally interesting. GANs can augment limited datasets by generating synthetic but realistic training examples, help design new molecules or materials with desired properties, or fill in missing data. In polymer science, GANs have been explored for generating synthetic kinetic data. In our own [bio-based polymer framework](https://github.com/PolymatGIQ/biobased), we implemented a GAN training mode for reactivity ratio prediction alongside the neural network and gradient boosting approaches, allowing the model to learn from the data distribution itself and generate plausible monomer pair interactions that extend beyond the training set. The ability to generate new data from a learned distribution is particularly valuable in fields where experimental data is limited and expensive to produce.

## Tree-Based Methods: The Other Side

Not everything in machine learning requires neural networks. In fact, for many practical problems involving structured, tabular data, the most effective methods are tree-based.

### Decision Trees and Random Forests

Decision trees are among the most interpretable models available. They make predictions by asking a sequence of yes/no questions about the input features, splitting the data at each step based on whichever feature and threshold best separates the outcomes. The result is a tree-like structure where you can literally trace the path from input to prediction and understand every decision the model made. "Is the temperature above 80°C? Yes. Is the monomer concentration below 2 mol/L? No. Then predict X." There is a clarity to this that neural networks simply cannot match.

This interpretability is a genuine advantage, especially in regulated industries or in situations where you need to explain your model's reasoning to a colleague, a regulator, or yourself. However, individual decision trees have a well-known weakness: they tend to overfit. A single tree will happily memorize every quirk of the training data, including noise, and perform poorly on new data.

Random forests solve this elegantly through ensemble learning. Instead of building one tree, we build a forest! hundreds or thousands of trees, each trained on a random subset of the data and a random subset of the features. Each tree makes its own prediction, and the final output is determined by majority vote (for classification) or averaging (for regression). The randomness ensures that the individual trees make different errors, and aggregating their predictions cancels out much of the noise. It is a beautifully simple idea, and in practice, random forests are remarkably robust and hard to beat on many tabular data problems.

### Gradient Boosting Methods

Gradient boosting takes the ensemble concept in a different direction. Instead of building many trees independently and averaging them (as in random forests), gradient boosting builds trees sequentially. Each new tree is specifically designed to correct the errors made by the ensemble so far.

Here is the intuition: you train your first tree, and it makes some predictions. Some of those predictions are wrong. The second tree focuses specifically on the cases where the first tree struggled, learning to predict the residual errors. The third tree corrects the remaining errors from the first two, and so on. Each new tree adds a small improvement, and the final prediction is the sum of all the trees' contributions. The "gradient" in gradient boosting refers to the fact that each new tree is fit to the negative gradient of the loss function, meaning it is trained to push the predictions in the direction that most reduces the overall error.

Modern implementations like XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost have become the go-to tools for tabular data problems. They are fast, handle missing values gracefully, and often outperform neural networks on structured datasets. In our work on [predicting multiple properties of bio-based monomers](https://doi.org/10.1002/aidi.202500248), we found that gradient boosting models were essential alongside neural networks, excelling particularly at predicting certain properties where the dataset structure favored ensemble tree methods over continuous function approximation.

In my experience, when you are working with process data in a spreadsheet rather than images or text, gradient boosting is always worth trying first. It will often give you an excellent baseline with minimal tuning, and it is surprisingly hard to beat.

### Support Vector Machines (SVMs)

Support vector machines approach classification from a geometric perspective. The core idea is to find the hyperplane that best separates two classes in feature space, where "best" means the hyperplane that maximizes the margin: the distance between the boundary and the nearest data points from each class. Those nearest points are the "support vectors," and they are the only data points that actually determine where the boundary goes. Everything else is irrelevant.

What makes SVMs particularly clever is the kernel trick. Real-world data is rarely linearly separable in its original feature space. The kernel trick allows SVMs to implicitly map the data into a much higher-dimensional space where a linear boundary does exist, without ever actually computing the coordinates in that high-dimensional space. Different kernel functions (linear, polynomial, radial basis function) correspond to different kinds of nonlinear boundaries in the original space.

SVMs were dominant in machine learning before deep learning took over, and they remain a strong choice for problems with small to medium-sized datasets, high-dimensional feature spaces, and clear margin-based separation between classes. In polymer science, SVMs have been used for tasks like classifying polymerization regimes, distinguishing between different types of degradation behavior, or identifying defective samples from quality control data. They are also mathematically elegant, which is a nice bonus if that sort of thing appeals to you. Where they struggle is with very large datasets and with problems where the class boundaries are highly irregular and complex, territories where deep neural networks tend to have the advantage.

## Beyond Off-the-Shelf: When You Need More

One thing I have learned from applying these methods in polymer reaction engineering is that off-the-shelf models, powerful as they are, sometimes are not enough. In science and engineering, we often have something that pure data-driven models do not account for: domain knowledge. We know the kinetic equations. We know conservation laws. We know that certain physical constraints must hold.

This realization led us to develop [polymer chemistry-informed neural networks](https://doi.org/10.1039/D4PY00995A), or PCINNs. Inspired by physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), the idea is to embed kinetic models and chemical knowledge directly into the training process of a neural network. Instead of treating the neural network as a black box that learns everything from data, you guide it with the science you already know. The network still has the flexibility to learn from data, but it is constrained to respect the underlying chemistry. The result is a model that needs less data, extrapolates more reliably, and produces predictions that are physically consistent, qualities that matter enormously when you are making decisions about real chemical processes.

We have demonstrated this for [solution polymerization](https://doi.org/10.1039/D4PY00995A), where PCINNs significantly outperformed both conventional neural networks and first-principles kinetic models, and for [emulsion polymerization](https://doi.org/10.1039/D4PY00995B), a notoriously complex system involving compartmentalized radical kinetics and colloidal phenomena. The emulsion PCINN was accurate enough and fast enough to serve as an on-the-fly model for real-time industrial applications. We are now extending this approach to RAFT polymerization, integrating active learning to use prediction uncertainty to guide the selection of the next most informative experiment.

This is where the field is headed, I think: not just applying ML models to scientific problems, but building models that genuinely incorporate scientific understanding. The methods described in this post are the building blocks. How you combine them, constrain them, and embed domain knowledge into them is where the real craft lies.

## Choosing the Right Tool

If there is one takeaway from this post, it is that there is no single best method. The right choice depends on your data, your problem, and your constraints. Neural networks are powerful and flexible, but they need lots of data and can be hard to interpret. Tree-based methods are robust and interpretable, but they struggle with high-dimensional continuous inputs like images or sequences. SVMs are elegant and effective for small datasets, but they do not scale as gracefully as other methods.

The practical reality is that most successful projects try several methods and compare. Start with a simple baseline (often gradient boosting for tabular data, or a small neural network for more complex inputs), see how it performs, and then iterate. Do not reach for the most complex architecture just because it sounds impressive. Reach for whatever answers the question most reliably with the data you actually have.
