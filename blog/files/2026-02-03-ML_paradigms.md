## Before We Start: What Is a Paradigm?

Before we talk about specific algorithms and architectures, it helps to understand the different *ways* a machine learning model can learn. Think of these paradigms not as competing approaches but as different philosophies of learning, each suited to different kinds of problems and different kinds of available data. The paradigm tells you how the model interacts with its data: does it get the answers during training? Does it figure things out on its own? Does it learn from consequences? The answer to that question shapes everything else.

## Supervised Learning

Supervised learning is the most intuitive paradigm, and it is where most people start their ML journey. The idea is simple: you have a dataset where both the inputs and the outputs are known, and you train a model to learn the mapping between them. The "supervision" comes from the fact that you are essentially showing the model the right answers during training, like a teacher grading homework. The model adjusts itself to reproduce those answers as closely as possible, and after enough practice, it should be able to generalize to examples it has never seen before.

Within supervised learning, there are two main flavors. In regression, the output is a continuous value. Think of predicting the melting temperature or the molecular weight of a polymer given the reaction conditions. You feed the model hundreds or thousands of examples where you already know the answer, and it learns a function that, ideally, generalizes to new situations. In classification, the output is a category. Instead of predicting a number, you are asking the model to sort things into bins: is this polymer thermoplastic or thermoset? Is the behavior rubbery or glassy?

The power of supervised learning is its directness. When you have well-labeled data, it is hard to beat. I experienced this firsthand when we trained a neural network on over 5,000 monomer pairs to predict [reactivity ratios in radical copolymerization](https://doi.org/10.1039/D3PY00246B). Reactivity ratios are notoriously difficult and time-consuming to measure experimentally, yet they govern copolymer composition and polymerization rate. With enough labeled examples (monomer structures as inputs, their known reactivity ratios as outputs), the supervised model significantly outperformed classical prediction methods that the polymer community had relied on for decades. More recently, we [extended this multi-property approach](https://doi.org/10.1002/aidi.202500248) to simultaneously predict propagation rate constants, reactivity ratios, glass transition temperatures, and water solubility for bio-based monomers, combining neural networks with gradient boosting to help accelerate the transition from petroleum-based to sustainable polymer systems.

But the limitation of supervised learning is equally direct: you need those labels. And in many real-world scenarios, especially in experimental sciences, labeled data is expensive, time-consuming, or simply scarce. Running polymerization experiments takes days. Characterizing a single sample can take weeks. You do not always have thousands of labeled examples lying around.

## Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised learning flips the script. Here, you hand the model a pile of data with no labels at all, and you ask it to find structure on its own. There is no "right answer" to learn from. Instead, the model looks for patterns, groupings, and relationships that exist naturally in the data.

Clustering is the classic example. Imagine you have measured dozens of properties for a large set of polymer samples, but you have no predefined categories. A clustering algorithm can look at all those measurements and say, "Based on the patterns I see, these samples naturally fall into three groups." You did not tell it how many groups to expect or what defines them. It figured that out from the data itself. This kind of analysis can be revelatory. A nice example of this in polymer science comes from a group that was working on reactivity ratios at the same time as us, but using [unsupervised learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19715). They applied spectral clustering to the physicochemical features of monomers, and without any labels, the clustering revealed which monomer combinations tend to produce alternating, random, block, or gradient copolymers. Seeing the same problem attacked from the unsupervised angle is a nice complement: the clustering gives you chemical insight and exploration, while supervised models give you the quantitative predictions you need for design.

Another important unsupervised technique is dimensionality reduction, sometimes called embedding. Real-world datasets often have many features, and visualizing or interpreting high-dimensional data is notoriously difficult for humans. We are good at reasoning in two or three dimensions, maybe four if we squint. But a polymer formulation might be described by dozens of variables: monomer concentrations, reaction temperatures, feed profiles, initiator types. Techniques like PCA (Principal Component Analysis) take your high-dimensional data and project it down into two or three dimensions while preserving as much of the meaningful variation as possible. What you get is a map of your data that you can actually look at and reason about. Points that are close together in this reduced space are similar in the original high-dimensional space, and clusters or outliers become visible.

Unsupervised learning is incredibly valuable for exploration and discovery. When you are at the beginning of a project and you do not yet know what questions to ask, unsupervised methods can reveal structure you did not know was there. They are also crucial as a component within larger pipelines. In one of our own projects on [reinforcement learning for reactor control](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compchemeng.2024.108739), we used an autoencoder (an unsupervised technique I will discuss in the next post) to compress distributions into a compact representation that the RL agent could work with. The unsupervised compression happened first; the supervised or reinforcement learning happened on top of it.

## Semi-Supervised Learning

Semi-supervised learning occupies the practical middle ground between supervised and unsupervised learning, and honestly, it reflects the reality of most real-world datasets better than either pure paradigm does. You have a small set of labeled data and a much larger set of unlabeled data, and you want to use both.

The intuition goes like this: even though most of your data points do not have labels, they still carry information about the underlying structure of the problem. The unlabeled data tells the model about the shape of the data distribution, where the clusters are, where the density concentrates, and where the gaps lie. The small set of labeled points then anchors those structures to actual categories or values.

In practice, this often works through a process called label propagation. You start with your few labeled examples, and the model gradually extends those labels to nearby unlabeled points based on similarity. If an unlabeled point sits squarely in the middle of a cluster of labeled "Class 1" points, it is reasonable to assume it is also Class 1. Then, once enough points have been pseudo-labeled, you can train a classifier on the whole enriched dataset. The key insight is that the unlabeled data is not wasted; it provides context that makes the small amount of labeled data go much further.

For anyone working in experimental sciences, this paradigm is particularly relevant. Running experiments is expensive. You might have thousands of unlabeled observations from routine process monitoring, things like temperature profiles, calorimetry traces, or spectral readings, but only a handful of carefully characterized samples where you actually measured the property of interest. Semi-supervised learning lets you leverage the full dataset without pretending you have more labels than you actually do. It respects the reality of your data situation, and that is a quality I appreciate in a methodology.

## Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning is fundamentally different from everything we have discussed so far. There is no dataset in the traditional sense. Instead, you have an agent that interacts with an environment, takes actions, and receives rewards or penalties. The agent learns through trial and error, gradually developing a policy (a strategy for choosing actions) that maximizes its cumulative reward over time.

The way I usually explain this is with a child learning to walk. No one teaches a toddler the biomechanics of bipedal locomotion. They just interact with the world. They fall, and it hurts (that's the penalty). They take a few wobbly steps and their parents erupt with excitement (that's the reward). Through this loop of trying, failing, and occasionally succeeding, they slowly build an internal sense of what works. That is reinforcement learning in its purest form.

Same thing in programming: at each time step, the agent observes the current state of the environment, selects an action based on its current policy, executes that action, and then receives a reward signal along with the new state. Over many iterations, the agent figures out which sequences of actions lead to the best outcomes.

What makes reinforcement learning powerful, and also challenging, is that the reward might be delayed. The agent might need to take a series of actions before it sees any payoff, and it has to figure out which of those earlier actions actually contributed to the eventual success or failure. This is known as the credit assignment problem, and it is one of the central puzzles in the field.

In my own work, reinforcement learning has become a central focus. The idea of training an AI agent to control emulsion polymerization reactors in real time is deeply compelling because these processes are exactly the kind of complex, dynamic environment where traditional control strategies struggle. We showed that RL agents can learn optimal control policies targeting [particle morphology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compchemeng.2024.108739) and [molecular weight distribution](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compchemeng.2026.109604) even when they are not directly observable, using a neural network as a state estimator to infer hidden characteristics from available measurements. The RL agent learned to optimize seed selection, feeding rates, and temperature simultaneously, and it could handle real-world disturbances like pump errors gracefully.

## Transfer Learning

Transfer learning is less of a learning paradigm in the philosophical sense and more of a pragmatic strategy, but it is so important in practice that it deserves its own section. The idea is to take a model that has already been trained on a large dataset for one task and repurpose it for a different but related task, typically one where you have much less data.

The reasoning is intuitive. Consider a concrete example from polymer science: we have a large dataset of reactivity ratios (over 5,000 monomer pairs from the Polymer Handbook), but for propagation rate constants (*kp*), the available data is much smaller. Since reactivity ratios are fundamentally ratios of propagation rate constants, a model trained on the large reactivity ratio dataset has already learned meaningful representations of monomer structure and how structural features relate to kinetic behavior. Instead of training a *kp* model from scratch on the small dataset (which would almost certainly overfit), you can take the pre-trained reactivity ratio model, keep most of its learned representations, and fine-tune just the final layers on the *kp* data. The pre-trained model brings its understanding of structure-reactivity relationships, and the small *kp* dataset teaches it the specifics.

Transfer learning is what makes it possible for small teams with modest datasets to benefit from models that cost millions of dollars and months of compute time to train originally. You are standing on the shoulders of a much larger training effort, and that is a perfectly good place to stand.

One related idea worth mentioning briefly is active learning, which is not quite a paradigm on its own but a strategy that can be layered on top of any of the paradigms above. The idea is to let the model tell you which experiment to run next. Instead of collecting data randomly, you use the model's own uncertainty to identify the data points where new measurements would be most informative. In my research stay at LCPO in Bordeaux, I worked on PCINNs for RAFT polymerization, using active learning to guide the selection of the next experiment based on where the ensemble of models disagrees most. It is a powerful way to squeeze more value out of every experiment, which matters enormously in fields where data collection is expensive.

## Wrapping Up

One thing I want to leave you with is that these paradigms are not mutually exclusive. In practice, the most effective machine learning pipelines combine ideas from multiple paradigms. You might use unsupervised clustering to explore your data, supervised learning to build a predictive model, transfer learning to bootstrap that model when your labeled data is limited, and reinforcement learning to optimize a process based on the model's predictions. The art is in knowing which combination fits the problem at hand, and that judgment comes with experience.
